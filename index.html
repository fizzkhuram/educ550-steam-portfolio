SECTION 01

1. Overview

This portfolio presents two connected but distinct STEAM case studies designed for one Grade 7 class within a four-week learning sequence. In Smart Shake, learners act as engineering designers who develop and test a protective base for a model building. In Market Marshes, they investigate how artificial intelligence can support flood forecasting by examining data, following an AI development lifecycle and considering the role of human judgment. The cases are sequenced to move learners from exploration and guided understanding towards collaborative application, evidence-based improvement, and increasingly independent reflection.

The two cases were selected because they make different dimensions of STEAM visible. Smart Shake foregrounds physical science, engineering design, measurement, and iterative prototyping. Market Marshes foregrounds environmental science, geography, data literacy, AI concepts, and responsible decision-making. Together, they demonstrate that STEAM is not simply the presence of five subject labels; it is the purposeful integration of disciplinary knowledge to investigate a problem and communicate a defensible solution (National Research Council, 2012).

The portfolio focuses exclusively on Grade 7. Development is examined longitudinally across four weeks of the same term, rather than by comparing different grade levels. The planned progression moves from supported exploration to guided application, collaborative problem-solving, and independent evaluation. Backward alignment connects intended outcomes to learning activities, formative checks, and final performance evidence. Students are assessed on reasoning, process, collaboration, and communication as well as the final product; this reduces the risk of rewarding only confident speakers, fast builders, or technically successful groups.

Portfolio design principles

  • Authentic purpose: each project addresses a recognizable challenge—structural safety or community flood preparedness.
  • Visible learning: sketches, annotated data, test logs, models, explanations, and exit responses make thinking observable.
  • Iterative practice: learners predict, test, interpret feedback, and revise rather than treating a first attempt as final.
  • Inclusive participation: tasks provide visual, spoken, written, and practical response routes, with scaffolded and extended pathways.
  • Responsible technology: digital tools support learning, but students examine model limitations, data quality, and human oversight.
  • Assessment for learning: feedback is timely, specific, and connected to a next action (Black & Wiliam, 1998).

2. Understanding developmental levels

This section considers the development of a Grade 7 class over four consecutive weeks within one term. Development is not treated as a fixed age-based ladder or as a claim that every learner progresses at the same pace. Instead, the sequence deliberately changes the balance of teacher guidance, task complexity, collaboration and learner independence. The teacher uses diagnostic responses, work samples, observation and formative assessment to decide when to maintain, reduce or reintroduce scaffolds. Progress is therefore evidenced through what learners can explain and do, not simply by the passage of time.

Week 1 – Explore and activate prior knowledge: learners connect each challenge to familiar experiences, interpret visual prompts, identify what they already know and record an initial prediction. The teacher makes key vocabulary visible, uses a short diagnostic task and models how to ask investigable questions. The emphasis is on curiosity, accessible entry points and surfacing misconceptions.

Week 2 – Build understanding through guided practice: learners develop the science, mathematics and digital concepts needed for each challenge. The teacher models a process aloud, breaks complex tasks into manageable steps and provides worked examples, checklists and structured choices. Learners begin to justify decisions using relevant concepts rather than intuition alone.

Week 3 – Apply, collaborate and test: learners take greater responsibility for planning and carrying out a practical investigation. They rotate roles, use agreed success criteria, collect or inspect evidence and respond to peer questions. Teacher prompts remain available, but groups are expected to make and explain more of their own decisions.

Week 4 – Improve, evaluate and transfer: learners use evidence to refine a design or reconsider a data/model decision, communicate a conclusion and identify a limitation. Scaffolds are reduced where formative evidence shows readiness. Each learner completes an individual reflection or explanation so that group achievement is not mistaken for individual understanding.

The progression is responsive rather than rigid. A learner who needs a vocabulary frame or worked example in Week 3 can still use it; another learner may be ready for a more open-ended comparison in Week 2. Universal Design for Learning is built in through multiple means of engagement, representation and action/expression (CAST, 2024). Learners may communicate through an annotated sketch, oral explanation or written response; instructions are both spoken and displayed; and individual checks accompany collaborative products.

4. Supportive Case Study One: Smart Shake

SMART SHAKE · ORIGINAL VISUALSEngineering concepts and prototype testing

4.1 Case context and authentic challenge

Case title: Smart Shake – Design, Build and Test an Earthquake-Resistant Building.

Intended learners: Grade 7. Time: two connected 50-minute lessons, positioned within the four-week term sequence.

Driving question: How can we design a protective base that reduces the shaking transferred to a model building while keeping it supported?

  
  

Learners work in small engineering teams to design a protective base for the same standard cardboard house (approximately 10 × 10 cm) mounted on a 25 × 25 cm board. The house and its mass remain unchanged so that the base design is the focus. The material allowance is limited to up to four marker rollers, two rubber bands, four cotton pads, 50 cm of tape and two pre-cut base-card pieces. Students use APE Earthquake Tester for digital exploration, Curipod or paper for a dimensioned design sketch, and a teacher-supervised physical test station for repeated trials.

The model is an educational simulation, not a certified building design. APE provides a simplified digital representation; the physical classroom test does not reproduce all features of an earthquake or real building. Students are explicitly taught that lower measured peak acceleration may be a useful comparison only when the house remains supported and the test is fair. It does not prove that a real building is earthquake-safe.

4.6 Case analysis: hypothetical classroom application

The following case is a hypothetical teaching scenario used to analyse how the plan could work; it is not a report of a completed lesson or actual student results. A mixed-readiness Grade 7 class is organised into small engineering teams. During the first lesson, one team initially proposes a rigid base because students assume that “stronger” must mean “less movement”. The teacher does not immediately reject the idea. Instead, the teacher asks students to predict what will happen when the board moves and directs them to compare the prediction with their APE observation. The group then sketches a base that allows limited movement while retaining support for the house.

During testing, the team’s first design produces inconsistent readings because the phone is placed differently between trials. The teacher pauses the group and returns to the fair-test checklist. Students identify sensor position as a variable that should be controlled and repeat the trials. They then change one feature and compare the initial and revised means. The important learning is not that the revised model must outperform the first one; it is that students can identify a testing weakness, improve the method and explain what the evidence does and does not show.

This scenario illustrates the value of formative questioning, explicit modelling and iterative design. It also reveals a practical risk: a single test station can create waiting time. The teacher can reduce this by preparing labelled kits, assigning meaningful observation or data-checking roles to waiting teams, and using a rotation schedule. Individual exit tickets are essential because a group’s successful explanation may conceal unequal understanding.

5.1 Case context and authentic challenge

Case title: Market Marshes—Understanding Flooding and Preparing Data for AI, followed by Training, Testing, and Using a Flood Prediction Model.

Intended learners: Grade 7

The supplied Google AI Quest is designed for Grade 7 and has an estimated 50-minute duration. This portfolio expands it into two connected 50-minute lessons so that students have time to understand data quality and the AI lifecycle before completing and reflecting on the quest.

Driving question: How can AI help a community prepare for a flood, and why must people remain involved when using an AI prediction?

Students consider flood impacts, identify rainfall and terrain as relevant data types, inspect a small fictional dataset, and explore the stages of data search, cleaning, selection, training, testing, and deployment. Testing may lead back to data selection when the model’s performance is weak. Students are taught that a forecast can support decisions but cannot prevent a flood or guarantee a correct prediction.

The supplied Market Marshes Teacher Guide identifies environmental science, geography, civics, and social studies as subject areas and describes how collecting data and training a model can help learners experience decisions made in AI development. The lesson adaptation adds teacher-created data-cleaning examples and formative checks; these examples are not data or results from the actual quest.

5.6 Case analysis: hypothetical classroom application

This is a hypothetical scenario for analyzing the planned approach, not a claim that a class completed the quest or achieved a particular score. A Grade 7 pair examines the fictional records and notices that one rainfall entry is missing. One learner suggests entering an estimated value so that the dataset appears complete. The teacher asks what evidence would justify that replacement and models the distinction between correcting a verified error and inventing a value. The pair flags the record for source checking and explains why the decision matters.

During the AI Quest, the pair reaches the testing stage and sees how predictions are compared with known outcomes. Rather than treating the model as an authority, the teacher asks learners to consider the consequences of a missed flood and a false alarm. Students then create a short message for a community audience: a forecast can inform preparation, but people should follow official guidance and understand that predictions contain uncertainty. If the online platform is unavailable, the teacher uses the explicitly labelled offline simulation and does not report it as actual quest performance.

The case demonstrates how data literacy and ethical reasoning can be integrated into a practical STEAM task. It also highlights two risks. First, students may confuse a game’s simplified representation with a complete real-world forecasting system. Second, device access can determine who controls the activity. The teacher addresses these risks by naming the model’s limits, rotating device roles, providing printed alternatives, and assessing individual explanations.

5.7 Critical evaluation of Market Marshes

  • Strength: the environmental context gives AI learning a social purpose and helps students connect data decisions to community preparedness.
  • Strength: the lifecycle sequence makes otherwise invisible AI processes discussable and gives students a vocabulary for data preparation, training, testing and deployment.
  • Strength: the data-cleaning task challenges the misconception that missing information should simply be guessed or that more data is always better.
  • Limitation: a short educational quest simplifies real forecasting systems, which use more complex datasets, modelling methods and validation. The teacher must make this simplification explicit.
  • Limitation: learners may over-trust a displayed prediction or treat one accuracy value as proof of reliability. Discussion of false alarms, missed events, data coverage and human oversight counters this.
  • Improvement: ask learners to compare how different communities may be represented in datasets and who could be disadvantaged when information is incomplete.
  • Improvement: collect individual exit evidence after group gameplay so that navigation success is not mistaken for conceptual understanding.

Innovative and engaging teaching methods

  1. Problem-based launch: introduce a meaningful challenge before teaching the technical vocabulary so learners have a reason to use new concepts.
  1. Digital exploration followed by physical or collaborative work: APE supports prediction and comparison before construction; Market Marshes makes the AI lifecycle visible before learners interpret its social implications.
  1. Choice within constraints: students choose design features or justify data decisions, while shared success criteria keep the task academically focused.
  1. Visible thinking routines: prediction–observation–explanation, think–pair–share, annotated sketches, data-quality flags and before/after comparisons.
  1. Peer review and role rotation: learners practise explaining, listening and improving ideas while the teacher checks that participation is distributed.
  1. Authentic communication: students explain a design to an engineering audience or communicate a forecast cautiously to a community audience.

Formative assessment and feedback cycle

In both cases, assessment is embedded throughout the learning sequence. The teacher first elicits prior knowledge, models a process, checks understanding during the task, provides feedback tied to a criterion and gives learners an opportunity to act on that feedback. The exit ticket supplies individual evidence that can shape the next lesson. This reflects the view that formative assessment is most useful when evidence is interpreted and used to adapt teaching and learning, rather than simply collected (Black & Wiliam, 1998).

SECTION 07

7. Critical reflection and professional improvement

Designing these two cases has reinforced my view that a strong STEAM lesson is not defined by the number of digital tools or the visual appeal of the final product. Its quality depends on whether students use relevant knowledge to make decisions, test those decisions and explain what the evidence means. Smart Shake makes this visible through physical constraints and repeated measurements. Market Marshes makes it visible through data choices, model testing and the need for human judgement.

A strength of the portfolio is the alignment between intended outcomes, learning activities and assessment evidence. For example, the Smart Shake outcome about fair testing is assessed through the controlled procedure and the trial log, not inferred from a successful model. The Market Marshes outcome about data quality is assessed through learners’ decisions about duplicates, missing values and invalid entries, not merely through completion of a digital game. This alignment should make feedback more precise and reduce the risk of assessing presentation skills when the intended outcome is scientific or computational reasoning.

A second strength is the deliberate four-week progression within one Grade 7 term. The sequence makes the intended shift in learning visible: learners begin by exploring and articulating prior ideas, then practise with modelling, apply concepts collaboratively and finally evaluate and communicate with greater independence. I would not remove scaffolds simply because a week has passed. Diagnostic responses, the quality of explanations and observed independence would determine whether a prompt is retained, adapted or withdrawn. UDL principles also remind me to plan access from the beginning rather than wait until a learner struggles (CAST, 2024).

There are also practical tensions. The Smart Shake testing protocol requires consistency, supervision and time; if the equipment is not prepared, the lesson may become a queue rather than an inquiry. The Market Marshes quest depends on digital access and may encourage learners to focus on progressing through screens instead of understanding the lifecycle. In both cases, a visually impressive outcome can mask weak individual understanding. My response is to prepare offline alternatives, use explicit role rotation, schedule checkpoints and collect individual evidence at the end.

A further responsibility is to communicate the limits of models. A classroom shake test cannot certify a building, and an educational AI quest cannot represent the full complexity of operational flood forecasting. If I overstate either model, I risk teaching students to confuse a simplified representation with reality. I therefore need to model cautious language, ask what evidence is missing and invite learners to identify what the model leaves out. This is especially important when a project touches public safety.

My next step would be to pilot each sequence with a small group, collect anonymised samples of sketches, test logs, data annotations and exit tickets, and ask a colleague to moderate a selection using the analytic rubrics. I would compare how consistently the criteria are interpreted, note where learners misunderstand instructions and revise the timing or scaffolds. I would also review whether the tasks provide equitable opportunities for students with different language, access and physical needs. These are proposed improvement actions; this portfolio does not claim that the pilot or moderation has already taken place.
